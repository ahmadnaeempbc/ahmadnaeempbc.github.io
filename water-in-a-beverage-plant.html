Water and utilities

Where the water goes in a beverage plant, and where to look first

By Muhammad Ahmad Naeem · 15 November 2026

At PepsiCo I delivered eleven water conservation projects that together save 306,000 cubic metres of water a year. None of them needed new technology. Most of the work was finding out where the water was really going. Here is the order I would follow on any beverage plant.

Start with a water balance, not a project list

Every plant knows its total intake. Far fewer know how that total splits between product, rinsing, cleaning, utilities and drain. Until you have that split, every water project is a guess. Put meters or temporary flow measurements on the big branches, and work out litres of water used per litre of beverage produced. That ratio is the number you manage.

Check the water treatment plant itself

Reverse osmosis rejects part of everything it treats, and filters use water every time they backwash. I commissioned a new reverse osmosis plant at PepsiCo, and the recovery setting and backwash frequency decide how much treated water you get from each cubic metre of raw water. Reject water is also often good enough for uses that do not touch product. It was our biggest recovery stream.

Look at rinsing and cleaning next

Bottle rinsers and cleaning in place cycles run all day, so small excesses add up. We routed rinser water back into treatment instead of to drain. Ask also whether rinse steps are ended by time or by measurement. A rinse that stops on conductivity uses only what it needs.

Recover what utilities throw away

Steam condensate is hot, clean water. Sending it to drain wastes water, heat and boiler chemicals together. Cooling water that passes through once and goes to drain is the same story, and converting it to a closed loop removes the loss completely.

Walk the floor for the small flows

Running hoses, leaking pump seals, conveyor lubrication and water pressure set for the largest pack and left there for the smallest are all easy to miss on a drawing and easy to see in person.

Lock in the saving

A saving that depends on someone remembering to close a valve will not last. Put the meter on a daily report, give the number an owner, hand it over to the operators properly and review it with the production figures.

The pattern across all eleven projects was the same. Measure the baseline, map the loss, design the simple fix, and make the result visible every day.
